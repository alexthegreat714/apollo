Title: Understanding the Income Statement
Date: 2025-03-12
Category: education
Source: Apollo Synthetic Finance Set
Priority: 4

An income statement, also called a profit and loss statement, reports a company's financial performance over a specific period. Unlike the balance sheet's snapshot approach, the income statement shows revenues and expenses accumulated during a quarter or year.

The basic structure follows:

Revenue
- Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= Gross Profit
- Operating Expenses
= Operating Income (EBIT)
- Interest Expense
= Earnings Before Tax (EBT)
- Income Tax
= Net Income

Numerical Example:

ABC Company Income Statement (Annual, in thousands):

Revenue: $2,000
Cost of Goods Sold: $1,200
Gross Profit: $800

Operating Expenses:
- Selling, General & Administrative: $300
- Depreciation: $100
Total Operating Expenses: $400

Operating Income (EBIT): $400

Interest Expense: $50
Earnings Before Tax: $350

Income Tax (25%): $87.5
Net Income: $262.5

Key profitability ratios:

Gross Profit Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue
= $800 / $2,000 = 40%

Operating Margin = Operating Income / Revenue
= $400 / $2,000 = 20%

Net Profit Margin = Net Income / Revenue
= $262.5 / $2,000 = 13.125%

Earnings Per Share (EPS) = Net Income / Shares Outstanding

If ABC has 100,000 shares outstanding:
EPS = $262,500 / 100,000 = $2.625

Important considerations when analyzing income statements:

1. Revenue recognition policies affect timing of reported income
2. Non-recurring items should be separated from core operations
3. Comparing margins across periods reveals operational trends
4. Industry benchmarks provide context for margin analysis
5. EBITDA (adding back depreciation and amortization) approximates cash generation

Income statements connect to balance sheets through retained earnings and to cash flow statements through reconciliation of net income to operating cash flow.
