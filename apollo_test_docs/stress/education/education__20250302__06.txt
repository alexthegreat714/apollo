Title: Understanding Compound Interest
Date: 2025-03-02
Category: education
Source: Apollo Synthetic Finance Set
Priority: 4

Compound interest is the process by which interest is calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. This mechanism allows investments to grow exponentially over time, distinguishing it from simple interest which only calculates interest on the original principal.

The fundamental formula for compound interest is:

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

Where:
- A = Final amount
- P = Principal (initial investment)
- r = Annual interest rate (as decimal)
- n = Number of compounding periods per year
- t = Time in years

Numerical Example:

Consider an initial investment of $10,000 at an annual interest rate of 6%, compounded monthly, for 5 years.

P = $10,000
r = 0.06
n = 12 (monthly compounding)
t = 5 years

A = 10,000(1 + 0.06/12)^(12 × 5)
A = 10,000(1 + 0.005)^60
A = 10,000(1.005)^60
A = 10,000 × 1.3489
A = $13,489

The investment grows to $13,489, earning $3,489 in interest. Compare this to simple interest, which would yield only $3,000 (10,000 × 0.06 × 5).

The frequency of compounding affects the final amount. More frequent compounding periods result in higher returns. Daily compounding yields more than monthly, which yields more than annual compounding.

The Rule of 72 provides a quick estimation method: divide 72 by the interest rate to approximate the number of years required to double an investment. At 6% interest, approximately 12 years are needed to double the principal.

Understanding compound interest is essential for evaluating savings accounts, bonds, loans, and long-term investment strategies. The concept applies equally to debt, where compounding can accelerate the growth of amounts owed.
